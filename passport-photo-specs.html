Passport and ID photos: the rules, and how to satisfy them

Most rejected photos fail on four things, none of them the camera. Here is what the checks look for, what to fix before you submit, and where to confirm each country's exact numbers.

By FitMyPic Editorial Published 2026-10-22 Last reviewed 2026-10-22 How we test

At a glance

  • Almost all passport photos follow one standard shape: either 35 x 45 mm or a 2 x 2 inch square, on a plain light background, with the head filling most of the frame.
  • The most common rejection reasons are shadows behind the head, a background that is not plain, an expression or accessory that is not allowed, and a photo that has been digitally altered.
  • Many authorities explicitly reject photos that have been edited, including background removal and filters. Software should crop and resize, not retouch.
  • Resolution matters less than proportion: the head must be the right share of the frame.
  • Numbers on this page are sourced; we re-checked the UK figures against gov.uk on 22 October 2026 and link every authority's own page so you can confirm the current rule.

Why photos get returned

A passport or visa photo is not judged on how good it looks. It is checked against a short list of measurable properties, and it is usually rejected by software or by a clerk with a ruler before any human considers it as a photograph. In practice the same four failures account for most returns:

  1. Shadows. A shadow behind the head or beside the face reads as a non-uniform background, even when the wall is white. Standing half a metre away from the wall instead of against it removes almost all of them.
  2. An uneven or non-plain background. Curtains, doors, door frames, plug sockets, a second person, a pet. A plain wall — or a sheet hung flat — is the reliable option.
  3. Framing and proportion. Head too small, head too large, uneven margins, tilted frame. Authorities specify not just the paper size but how much of the frame the face occupies.
  4. Editing and presentation. Filters, retouching, and often background replacement. Plenty of authorities say in plain terms that digitally altered photographs will be rejected, and some will treat it as a misrepresentation. Cropping and resizing is expected; beauty processing is not.

Add two smaller categories: accessories (glasses, hats, head coverings worn without a religious or medical reason) and expression (open mouth, smiling with visible teeth in some systems, eyes not clearly visible).

The sizes the systems use

There are effectively two families. The 35 x 45 mm portrait format used across much of Europe and Asia, and the 2 x 2 inch square used by the United States and a few others. Everything else — background colour, head height, whether glasses are allowed — is national detail layered on top.

Photographing yourself at home

A phone camera in good light, used carefully, produces a perfectly acceptable passport photo. The technique matters more than the device.

  1. Stand about 50 cm away from a plain, light wall. Not against it — distance is what eliminates the shadow that a wall casts behind your head.
  2. Use even, indirect light. Facing a window in daylight works well; a ceiling light directly overhead does not, and a camera flash creates a hard shadow and reflective highlights on skin.
  3. Hold the phone at eye level and shoot straight on, not from below. A low angle changes the shape of the face and shifts the apparent head height.
  4. Do not use the wide-angle front camera if the phone has a better rear camera. Wide lenses at close range enlarge the nose and distort the face outline. Ask someone to take the photo, or use a timer and step back.
  5. Neutral expression, mouth closed, eyes open and clearly visible, hair away from the eyebrows and eye corners. Remove glasses if the rules allow it, or the local rules permit — this is now the default in several systems.
  6. No filters, no beauty mode, no portrait-mode blur. Portrait mode fakes the very background uniformity the photo is checked for, and the edge artefacts around hair are exactly what automated checks flag.

Take a dozen frames, then pick the one where the head is upright, the background is even, and nothing is clipped.

Cropping to the required pixels

Once you have a frame you like, the job is arithmetic rather than art:

  1. Determine the required pixel dimensions. For an online upload, use the authority's own minimum — for example 600 x 750 pixels for a UK online application. For print, use the physical size at 300 PPI: 35 x 45 mm becomes 413 x 531 pixels.
  2. Crop to the correct aspect ratio first. Crop, do not stretch: a face that has been stretched is immediately recognisable as a manipulated image.
  3. Position the head inside the frame with the specified margins — for 35 x 45 mm formats the head typically occupies 70-80% of the height, with a little space above it.
  4. Resize to the final pixel dimensions with a high-quality filter (bicubic or Lanczos). The browser resizer in this site does this in one step.
  5. Export as JPEG at high quality, well inside the file-size range the authority allows, and check the result at 100% zoom before submitting.

Before you submit

  • The photo is recent, in colour, and unaltered apart from cropping and resizing.
  • Background is plain and even, with no shadow behind the head or beside the face.
  • Head upright, filling the specified share of the frame, with even margins.
  • No glasses, no hat, no filter, no beauty retouching, no background replacement.
  • The pixel dimensions match the requirement exactly, and the file sits inside the accepted size range.
  • The file is a standard format — JPEG for uploads, glossy print for postal applications — and you kept the original in case a re-submission is requested.

If a photo fails twice for reasons you cannot identify, a passport photo booth or a local photographer is cheap insurance. Their output is already sized for the local authority, which removes the entire class of framing and proportion errors.
