Social media image sizes: what the platforms actually say

Every week a blog republishes a table of social media sizes with no date on it. This page quotes the platform help centres instead, and tells you what to do when they change.

By FitMyPic Editorial Published 2026-10-22 Last reviewed 2026-10-22 How we test

At a glance

  • Instagram's help centre states it uploads at a maximum width of 1080 pixels; photos between 320 and 1080 pixels wide keep their resolution only if the aspect ratio is between 1.91:1 and 3:4.
  • Outside that ratio range, Instagram crops the photo to a supported ratio rather than distorting it.
  • YouTube's thumbnail guidance asks for the largest image you have — 3840 x 2160 for videos, 2160 x 3840 for Shorts, minimum 640 pixels on the short side.
  • Thumbnail upload limits are 2 MB on mobile and 50 MB on desktop, accepted as JPG or PNG.

What the platforms themselves specify

Recommended-size tables circulate widely, and most of them are undated copies. The two entries below come from the platform's own help pages, read on 22 October 2026:

Platform Surface What the platform says Source
Instagram Photo uploads Uploads at a maximum width of 1080 px. Images 320-1080 px wide keep their original resolution only if the aspect ratio is between 1.91:1 and 3:4 (width 1080, height 566-1440). Unsupported ratios are cropped. Narrower than 320 px is upscaled; wider than 1080 px is downscaled. Instagram Help Centre, "Photo resolution when sharing to Instagram"
YouTube Video thumbnails "As large as possible": 3840 x 2160 for videos, 2160 x 3840 for Shorts, minimum width or height 640 px. JPG or PNG. 16:9 for videos, 9:16 for Shorts. Upload limit 2 MB on mobile, 50 MB on desktop. YouTube Help, "Add custom thumbnails"

For X, LinkedIn, Facebook and TikTok, the platforms publish their own current figures in their help centres. We link them below rather than repeat numbers we could not re-check against the source on the same day, because these pages change without notice and a stale table is worse than none.

Instagram: the 1080 pixel rule, and why crops happen

Instagram's own description is unusually precise, and it explains most upload surprises:

  • Wider than 1080 px — the platform shrinks the photo to 1080 px wide. Uploading a 4000 px photo is not harmful, but it is not what you see afterwards either.
  • 320-1080 px wide with a supported ratio — the original resolution survives. The supported range is 1.91:1 (wide landscape) to 3:4 (tall portrait), which at 1080 px wide corresponds to a height between 566 and 1440 px.
  • Outside that ratio — the platform crops to a supported ratio. This is why a very tall panorama loses its edges after upload.
  • Narrower than 320 px — the platform enlarges it to 320 px, which softens it further.

The practical rule that follows: export at 1080 px on the long edge, inside the 1.91:1-3:4 window, and let the platform do nothing. Useful dimensions inside that window, computed from the stated range:

Ratio At 1080 wide Typical surface
1:1 1080 x 1080 Square feed post
4:5 1080 x 1350 Tall feed post
3:4 1080 x 1440 Tallest ratio Instagram keeps uncropped
1.91:1 1080 x 566 Widest ratio Instagram keeps uncropped
9:16 1080 x 1920 Stories and Reels territory

Anything wider than 1.91:1 or taller than 3:4 is cropped, so if a composition depends on the extreme edges, keep the subject inside the 1080 x 1080 centre and treat the rest as margin.

YouTube: thumbnails, and a table that has aged badly

The frequently repeated advice — "upload a 1280 x 720 thumbnail under 2 MB" — is older than the current help page. YouTube now asks for the largest image available, with a 640 pixel minimum, and states the upload limits per device: 2 MB from mobile, 50 MB from desktop.

Two consequences worth knowing:

  1. Pick the ratio before the pixels. 16:9 for standard videos and 9:16 for Shorts; the ratio that matters is the one the surface displays.
  2. Upload from a computer when the file is large. A 2 MB mobile ceiling will reject a 3 MB PNG that the desktop flow accepts without complaint.

Text legibility is the other thing thumbnails are judged on. Since the platform may show the thumbnail at small sizes, keep any text large relative to the frame and avoid depending on details that vanish when the image is scaled down.

Why published tables disagree with each other

Three reasons, and none of them mean the table is a lie:

  • Upload size versus display size. Platforms store what you upload within limits, then render it at whatever size the surface uses. A table quoting "1080 x 1350" may be describing what the app displays, not what it accepts.
  • Crop ratios versus file dimensions. Some guides list ratios (4:5), others list pixels (1080 x 1350). Both can be right; they are different columns of the same table.
  • Version drift. Requirements change with app versions and surface redesigns, and unmaintained tables do not.

So the durable skill is not memorising a table; it is knowing the three-step test that any platform's requirement reduces to.

The workflow that satisfies any platform

  1. Find the current requirement on the platform's own help centre — not on a blog. Search the help centre for the feature name plus "size" or "resolution".
  2. Match the aspect ratio first. Cropping to the correct ratio is free; fixing a wrong ratio later is not. If the ratio must change, crop rather than stretch.
  3. Then match the pixel dimensions. Resize once, from the largest source, with a quality-oriented filter — nearest-neighbour downscaling leaves 6.85 levels of halftone noise where a proper filter leaves 1.18, and its output even compresses worse afterwards.
  4. Then respect the byte limit, if the surface enforces one. For images with text overlays, use PNG; for photographs, JPEG in the 75-85 quality band is the efficient choice. If the limit is tight, reduce the dimensions further rather than driving quality below 70.
  5. Check the export at 100% before uploading, especially on the edges where text and logos sit.
