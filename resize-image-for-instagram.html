What this Instagram preset does

The tool creates a real 1080 × 1080 image rather than only changing the preview. Crop to fill uses the whole square without distorting the subject. Fit entire image keeps every edge and adds canvas space when the source is portrait or landscape.

Crop or fit: which should you choose?

Crop works well for centered portraits, product photos, and artwork with clear space around the subject. Choose fit when text, logos, prices, or product edges must remain visible. Set the canvas color before exporting a fitted JPG.

Check the image before posting

Compare Original and Result to inspect framing and fine text. A square post can also appear as a smaller profile-grid tile, so avoid placing essential details directly against an edge. The measured output line confirms dimensions, format, and encoded file size.

Instagram image questions

Is 1080 × 1080 still useful for Instagram?

Yes. It remains a practical square format for feed graphics, product posts, announcements, and images designed to read consistently in a profile grid.

Will this tool stretch my photo?

No. Crop trims the longer dimension and fit adds canvas space. Both preserve the source proportions.

Should I use JPG or PNG?

JPG is usually smaller for photography. PNG is useful for graphics, screenshots, sharp text, or transparency. WebP is available when the destination accepts it.

What Instagram does to the file you upload

Instagram's help centre is explicit about the pipeline: uploads are handled at a maximum width of 1080 pixels, photos between 320 and 1080 pixels wide keep their resolution only if the aspect ratio falls between 1.91:1 and 3:4, and anything outside that window is cropped to a supported ratio. Below 320 pixels wide, the platform enlarges the image to 320.

Three practical consequences follow from that single paragraph.

Uploading a 4000-pixel photo buys nothing. It is downscaled to 1080 before anyone sees it. The bytes you spend above 1080 pixels wide serve the platform's pipeline, not your viewer.

The safest export is 1080 pixels on the long edge, inside the ratio window. At 1080 wide the window is a height between 566 and 1440 pixels, which covers square posts, 4:5 tall posts and the widest acceptable landscape.

Ratios outside the window are cropped, not distorted. The composition does not stretch; it loses edges. If something important sits near the border — a caption, a logo, a face — it may not survive.

Check the ratio before the pixel count

Changing ratio is a crop decision and it is free; changing pixel count after the fact is a second resample. Decide the ratio first, then the dimensions.

The 9:16 figure sits outside the feed window quoted above; feed posts and vertical full-screen surfaces are handled differently, so treat the table as per-surface rather than one universal size.

Margins, not accuracy, save a crop

Rather than matching a ratio to three decimal places, keep the subject inside the centre square and treat the outer tenth of the frame as expendable. Any surface that re-crops takes from the edges first.

Why a re-exported photo looks worse on Instagram

If you take a photo, resize it, save it as JPEG, then open the export and resize it again, you have run two interpolations and two encodings. Each pass removes information that the next one cannot recover.

The measured version of that claim: on a 4x downscale, a quality-oriented filter (bicubic) scored 46.60 dB against an area-average reference with SSIM 0.9856, while nearest-neighbour scored 31.66 dB and SSIM 0.6028 and left 6.85 levels of halftone noise. Run that twice and the second pass starts from already-degraded input.

The fix is procedural: work from the original file, resize once, export once.

A workflow that survives the pipeline

  1. Start from the largest original you have — the camera file or a full-resolution export.
  2. Crop to the target ratio at full resolution.
  3. Resize to 1080 on the long edge with a quality-oriented filter. Avoid nearest-neighbour unless the image is pixel art.
  4. Only compress if the file needs to be smaller; for most photos, a single JPEG export in the 75-85 quality band from a 1080-pixel canvas lands comfortably under typical limits.
  5. Inspect the export at 100% zoom on the busiest area before uploading.
