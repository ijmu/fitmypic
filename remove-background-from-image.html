Two removal modes

Connected edges vs. every matching pixel

Connected to edges (default)

Removes only background regions touching the image border. Safer when the subject contains a similar color — a white shirt on a white backdrop keeps its shirt.

Every matching pixel

Removes all pixels matching the sampled color anywhere in the image. Use it for cutouts with holes (between an arm and torso) or repeated background color in separate areas.

Questions

Background removal FAQ

Is the result really transparent?

Yes. The exported PNG contains a genuine alpha channel. The checkerboard in the preview is only a convention for showing transparency.

What kind of images work best?

Photos with solid or near-solid backgrounds: white studio product shots, flat-color backdrops, and lightly uneven tones. Busy or highly detailed backgrounds need professional AI segmentation tools.

What do tolerance and edge feather do?

Tolerance decides how similar a pixel must be to the sampled color to become transparent. Feather softens the transition at edges so cutouts don't look harshly outlined.

Can I get a white background instead of transparent?

Yes. Choose solid white, solid black, or a custom color in the Replace with menu — feathered edges blend smoothly into the new background.

Are my photos uploaded?

No. Everything runs locally in your browser with the canvas API. Your images are never transmitted to a server.

When colour-based removal works, and when it cannot

Removing a background by colour matching is a bet: the algorithm assumes the subject is separable from what surrounds it because the two differ in colour. That bet pays off in a predictable set of cases and fails in an equally predictable set.

It works well on: images photographed against a plain, evenly lit surface (product shots on white paper, a person against a painted wall), logos and flat illustrations on a single colour, scans of documents on clean backing, and screenshots of graphics where the background is one fill.

It fails, or needs manual work, on: gradient backgrounds and studio lighting with shadows, hair and fur, semi-transparent objects (glass, smoke, veils, tulle), reflective or shiny edges picking up the background colour, busy surroundings, and anything where the subject shares colour with the background.

Half-transparent pixels are the boundary case worth understanding. An antialiased edge contains pixels that are genuinely mixed between subject and background. A hard threshold has to decide they are one or the other, and that decision is where the ugly border comes from.

Why the edges get a halo

The white fringe that appears around a cutout is almost always one of three things.

Trapped background pixels. The alpha mask stops one or two pixels short of the actual subject boundary, so a thin band of background colour survives at the edge. Against a different backdrop the band reads as a rim of the old background.

Colour bleed from a scaled image. If the source was resized or compressed with JPEG before the cutout, edge pixels carry colours blended toward the background. Those blends survive as a coloured halo even when the alpha is correct.

A matte colour that does not match the destination. When a transparent image is composited onto white, any subtle semi-transparent edge tint shows. The same file placed on a dark background may look perfectly clean.

Fixes, in order of effort: reduce tolerance so fewer background pixels are kept; add a slight feather so the boundary is a gradient rather than a hard line; and inspect the result against both a white and a dark checkerboard before shipping.

The preview background matters

Check the cutout over two different backgrounds. An edge that looks perfect on white can show a dark halo on a dark surface, and vice versa. This is the failure mode that is easiest to miss and hardest to diagnose later.

Removing versus choosing the right source

The single largest quality gain usually comes before any tool touches the file: start from the uncompressed original. Edge pixels in a JPEG that has already been re-encoded at quality 60 have colour bleed built in that no tolerance setting can undo.

Source Expected quality of a colour-based cutout
Original PNG or high-quality export, plain background Clean edges, minimal halo
JPEG q80+ on a plain background Good at normal viewing sizes, some fringing at 100%
Heavily recompressed JPEG, or a screenshot of a photo Visible fringing that survives every setting
Photo with shadow or gradient background Requires manual masking, not colour matching

Scenarios where a different route is correct

Some jobs look like background removal but are not:

  • Ecommerce listings that require a white background. If the marketplace specifies pure white, keep the background and simply make sure it is pure white — a cutout on white is not the same thing as a properly exposed white sweep, and some platforms reject images with visible cutout artefacts.
  • Official documents and identification photographs. Many authorities state the photo must not be digitally altered beyond cropping and compression. Producing a cutout and pasting it onto a white field can get a submission rejected even when it looks correct.
  • Complex subjects with important edges. Hair, fur, glass and motion blur are the cases where a mask needs to be drawn by hand; the automated route produces halos that cannot be tuned away.
  • Transparent objects. A colour-key cutout on glass removes the very gradients that make glass look like glass.
A two-minute sanity test

Zoom to 200% on the subject's edge and look for the old background's colour. If it is there, lower the tolerance and re-export. If it survives at the lowest setting that still removes the background, the image needs manual masking.

After the cutout

  1. Export as PNG. JPEG has no alpha channel at all, so a transparent result saved as JPEG gets flattened onto whatever matte colour the encoder chooses.
  2. Check the file over a white background and a dark one.
  3. Confirm the pixel dimensions still match the destination's requirement.
  4. Keep the original. Background removal is destructive and re-running it from the original is faster than repairing an edge.
